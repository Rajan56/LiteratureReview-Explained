A literature review is…

“a study that analyzes and synthesizes an existing body of literature by identifying, challenging, and advancing the building blocks of a theory through an examination of a body (or several bodies) of prior work”

Post et al. (2020, p. 352), as quoted in Kraus et al. (2022)

In practice it is secondary research: the data are published studies, and the method is how you find, select, appraise and synthesise them.

1 · Foundations

Why every research project needs a literature review

A review is not a formality before the "real" research. It decides which question is worth asking, which theory and methods are credible, and what your contribution can be .

A

Empirical research article

The review section positions the study: it defines constructs, justifies hypotheses or propositions from prior evidence, and shows the gap the study fills. Typically compact and argument-led.

B

Bachelor's or master's thesis

Shows you can find, organise and critically connect the literature, and derive a framework and research questions from it. Examiners look for synthesis, not summaries.

C

Doctoral dissertation

Demonstrates mastery of the field, its theories and methods; Boote and Beile (2005) call it the foundation of being a "scholar before researcher". Often one chapter becomes a published systematic review .

D

Standalone review article

A study in its own right: it can provide an overview of current knowledge, evaluate how knowledge has progressed, and propose pathways to advance it . Many journals publish only reviews.

      7 · Meta-analysis
      Meta-analysis: combining effect sizes, in detail
      
      
      A meta-analysis is the statistical part of a systematic review: it pools comparable effect sizes into one estimate, measures how much studies disagree, and tests whether the picture is distorted . All analyses below run in real Python in your browser.
      
    
      Python engine not loaded yet
      

    
      
      
      When to meta-analyse
      • Studies address the same question with comparable designs, populations and outcomes.
      • Each study reports an effect size or the statistics to compute one (means and SDs, events, correlations).
      • You have at least two studies, and in practice enough to estimate heterogeneity meaningfully.
      When not to
      • Studies are conceptually too different ("apples and oranges").
      • Outcomes are measured in incompatible ways or effect sizes cannot be derived.
      • Evidence is at high risk of bias: pooling biased studies gives a precise wrong answer. Use a structured narrative synthesis (SWiM) instead .
      
      
      Effect sizes
      • Standardised mean difference (Hedges' g) for two-group comparisons on continuous outcomes, with small-sample correction .
      • Log risk ratio / log odds ratio for binary outcomes.
      • Correlation r, pooled on Fisher's z and transformed back.
      • Rough benchmarks for g: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large ; judge against your field.
      
      
      Fixed (common) vs random effects
      A common-effect model assumes one true effect; differences are sampling error. A random-effects model assumes true effects vary across settings with variance τ², which is usually more realistic in social and management research . The classic DerSimonian-Laird estimator  is simple, while REML estimates τ² better ; the Hartung-Knapp adjustment gives more honest confidence intervals, especially with few studies .
      
      
      Heterogeneity
      Q tests whether studies differ more than chance; I² is the share of variability due to real differences; τ² is the variance of true effects; the prediction interval shows where the effect in a new setting would fall . Explore it with subgroups and meta-regression on moderators coded during extraction.
      
      
      Publication bias and small-study effects
      Significant results are more likely to be published . Inspect the funnel plot, test asymmetry with Egger's regression , and use trim-and-fill as a sensitivity analysis . Best protection: search grey literature, registries and preprints.
      
      
      Robustness and certainty
      Leave-one-out and cumulative analyses show whether one study drives the result. Rate risk of bias with RoB 2 or ROBINS-I  and the overall certainty of evidence with GRADE . Report with PRISMA 2020 .

      8 · Academic writing
      Synthesis: descriptive, analytical and evaluative writing
      
      
      Synthesis means combining sources into an argument, not reporting them one by one . Academic review writing moves through three levels, which mirror the higher levels of Bloom's revised taxonomy (analyse, evaluate, create) .

        9 · Analysing secondary data
        From a pile of papers to findings
        
      
        In a review, the included studies are your data. Analysis means extracting, coding and relating them, using objective methods (bibliometrics, counts), subjective methods (thematic and critical analysis), or both .
        

    
        
      
        1. Extraction form
        Pilot a form on 3–5 studies, then extract consistently: citation, country and sector, sample, design, theory, constructs and measures, key findings, limitations, quality score. Double-extract a sample to check reliability.
        
      
        2. Coding
        Deductive: codes come from a framework (e.g., TCCM, ADO) chosen in advance . Inductive: codes emerge from the texts, then are grouped into first-order concepts, second-order themes and aggregate dimensions .
        
      
        3. Concept matrix
        A study × concept table makes synthesis concept-centric rather than author-centric . Read down a column to write about a concept across studies.
        
      
        4. Thematic synthesis
        Three stages: line-by-line coding, descriptive themes, analytical themes that go beyond the original studies . For conflicting quantitative results without pooling, follow SWiM .
        
      
        5. Bibliometric analysis
        Performance analysis: productivity and impact of authors, sources, countries. Science mapping: co-citation, bibliographic coupling, co-word and co-authorship networks . Interpret clusters by reading their core papers.
        
      
        6. Quality appraisal
        Match the tool to the design: RoB 2 (randomised trials), ROBINS-I (non-randomised), CASP and JBI (qualitative), MMAT (mixed sets), AMSTAR 2 (for reviews in umbrella reviews) .

        10 · Tools
        Tools for each stage
        
      
        Most stages can be done with free tools. Whatever you use, record versions and settings so the review can be reproduced.
        
    
        
    
        On AI tools. Machine-learning prioritisation for screening is well established when the stopping rule is reported . Generative AI can help with brainstorming search terms and drafting, but it can invent references and misread studies: verify everything against the source and disclose its use according to your institution's and journal's policy.
